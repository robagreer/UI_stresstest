About the project

A common stress test for state unemployment-insurance funds

The Unemployment Insurance Stress Test is an interactive research tool for exploring how an employment-displacement scenario could affect each state UI trust fund. Each jurisdiction maintains its own fund, and reserves are not shared across state lines. The tool applies one transparent accounting framework to all 50 states and the District of Columbia, using published U.S. Department of Labor data to compare relative fiscal vulnerability.

It is a planning model, not a probability forecast. “Depletion year” means the first modeled year in which a fund balance reaches zero. A depleted fund does not stop paying benefits: the state borrows from the U.S. Treasury under Title XII and repays through higher employer taxes, so depletion marks the onset of federal borrowing, not the end of benefits. Jurisdictions already carrying federal loans are identified separately.

How exposure-weighted allocation works

The common scenario deliberately holds exposure constant to isolate fiscal structure. An exposure-weighted scenario instead redistributes the same national number of displaced workers according to each state's employment-weighted occupational exposure. Factors are normalized using covered employment, so the national shock total does not change. Exposure is used only to allocate the scenario—not to predict how many jobs AI will eliminate.

What this tool can and cannot tell you

It can compare the relative fiscal vulnerability of state UI funds under a common, transparent accounting scenario, and show how outcomes depend on fiscal structure (wage base, reserves, baseline cash flow) and on the policy response. It cannot predict whether, when, or how much AI displaces workers. Displacement size, take-up, reabsorption, and the baseline are scenario assumptions, and the exposure measures only allocate a fixed national scenario across states: they do not convert exposure into job loss, say anything about timing, or identify which occupations within a state are affected. All results are conditional on a scenario occurring.

Key terms for readers new to UI financing
Trust fund
Each jurisdiction pays UI benefits from its own dedicated fund, financed by state payroll taxes on employers. Reserves are not shared across state lines.
Covered employment
Jobs subject to UI payroll tax. Both contribution revenue and benefit eligibility run through covered work.
Taxable wage base
The slice of each worker's annual wages that is taxed. States on the frozen federal minimum ($7,000) have little revenue capacity regardless of their tax rate.
Net reserve
The trust fund balance net of outstanding federal loans; negative for states already in debt.
AHCM
Average High Cost Multiple: reserves expressed as years of payouts at the historically highest benefit rate. 1.00 is the federal solvency benchmark.
Title XII loan
When a fund reaches zero, the state borrows from the U.S. Treasury. Benefits continue; repayment falls on employers through higher taxes and federal credit reductions.
Take-up
The share of displaced workers who ever receive a first benefit payment.
Reabsorption
The eventual share of displaced workers who return to covered employment, restoring the contribution base.
Model. Claims run off gross separations; contribution revenue runs off net covered employment. The reabsorption rate is the eventual share returning to covered work after a fixed two-year lag. Take-up is the share of displaced workers receiving a first payment. Cycle-neutral mode holds each fund's pre-shock annual inflow and outflow equal. Current run-rate mode uses reported 12-month revenue and annualizes the latest quarter's regular benefit payments at four times the quarterly value; it is not seasonally adjusted. The illustrative automatic-response mode applies common extended-benefit and solvency-schedule parameters; it is not a state-by-state statutory forecast.
